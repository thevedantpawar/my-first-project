The workbook's own A/B/C labels are email deliverability tiers, not fit tiers — its own column says so: “email domain matches clinic website” / “free provider, likely personal” / “corporate domain does not match website.” Nothing in it had been scored against the offer. That scoring is the work.

The three pools

Pool 1 — the qualified 58

58 emails · individually hand-written · Apollo-enriched

Tier A, has a phone, 4.0+ rating, 150+ reviews, deduped by domain with chains collapsed to one row. Every one carries employee count, revenue, corporate phone and LinkedIn from Apollo. Each email was written from that specific clinic's facts — its real closed days, review count, staff size and service mix. Send these first.

Pool 2 — the 100-review band

78 emails · programmatically generated · not enriched

Same quality bar except the review floor drops from 150 to 100. Real businesses, just smaller. Copy is generated from each lead's actual numbers and closed days with rotating phrasing — better than a mail merge, but it is not the bespoke writing Pool 1 got. Enriching these would cost 78 credits and needs your say-so.

Pool 3 — Tier B owner inboxes

59 emails · programmatically generated · needs a separate sending domain

Of 160 Tier B leads, 59 clear the same 4.0-rating, 100-review, has-phone bar. These are gmail and yahoo addresses — which for a solo med spa is usually the owner, a better contact than info@. The copy is deliberately shorter and plainer, because it lands in a personal inbox rather than a front desk queue.

The generated copy cites each clinic's real rating, review count, city and actual closed days, and rotates opener, body and closing line so no two are identical. That is roughly level-2 personalization. It is not level-4 researched personalization, and the cold-email spec is explicit that templates with swapped fields underperform — so the 30-second human pass on Pools 2 and 3 is not optional polish, it is the difference between a reply and a spam report.

What is still missing

The SEO audit never happened

This was the original request and it is still outstanding. The session's network policy blocked page fetching for every domain in the list — a control fetch of example.com failed the same way. Title tags, canonicals, robots.txt, sitemaps, Core Web Vitals, schema and alt text all require loading the pages.

No findings were invented to fill the gap. Re-run from a session with open egress, or paste page source and it can be audited against the same rubric.

No decision-maker names — all 195 emails open without a greeting

Apollo's people search returns API_INACCESSIBLE on the Free plan. people_match is reachable but enriches a person you can already name; it cannot answer “who runs this clinic?” from a domain. Credits do not solve this — it is a plan tier.

Upgrading is the single highest-leverage change available: it converts 195 unaddressed sends into 195 addressed ones. Every email is written to accept a greeting line without rework.

Nothing is verified against the live sites

Every claim traces to the workbook or to web search, never to the clinic's own page. Two load-bearing ones to confirm first: Kumi's BBB C+ rating, and Suddenly Slimmer's platform rating split. Both anchor their emails.

Three email domains in the source are wrong and will bounce as supplied — the real ones are btyco.com, southbaymedicalspa.com and artistikbeauty.net. Apollo also disagrees with the workbook on several cities, and one franchise contact carries an Arizona address against a Florida listing.

What enrichment changed

Headcount re-segmented the qualified 58 in a way review counts could not see. Two Pool 1 leads originally ranked P1 turned out to be micro shops — The Palm has 2 employees, Kumi has 4 — and Elase, pitched as a four-location chain, is a 230-person national platform across 38 locations. That email would have read as though nobody had looked them up.

Send sequence

  1. Fix the three bad domains and spot-check the Kumi and Suddenly Slimmer claims. Half an hour, and it protects every send after it.
  2. Send the 26 Core and Small leads first. Best fit, hand-written, highest reply probability. This is the batch that tells you whether the offer lands.
  3. Then the rest of Pool 1 — enterprise on the pilot framing, micro on the single-automation pitch. Do not send the micro shops the full stack.
  4. Read and send Pool 2 in batches of 20, editing as you go. What you change by hand is signal about what the generator got wrong.
  5. Warm a second domain, then run Pool 3. Personal inboxes punish cold domains harder than business ones.
  6. Decide on Apollo. A paid tier buys decision-maker names for all 195; 78 more credits enriches Pool 2. The first is worth more than the second.
