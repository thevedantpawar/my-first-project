All 804 first emails are hand-written. Each opens on that clinic’s own facts — real closed days, review count, rating, staff size, service mix, and in a dozen cases the honest admission that the offer does not fit them. Subjects are two to four lowercase words; bodies run 8 to 107 words, shortest at the bottom of the list where an owner reads their own inbox between clients.

Touches 2 through 5 are segment-level, not individual, and that is on purpose. The directives require each follow-up to add one new value proposition and never repeat the first email’s argument. At 739 sends the reliable way to do that is a library keyed to the angle touch 1 used — eight segments: coverage, reviews, qualification, membership, packages, multi-site, early, and wrong-fit. The twelve wrong-fit leads get one touch and no follow-up at all; following up on “this probably isn’t for you” turns a courtesy into spam.

What the second enrichment run changed

A scoring flaw buried your best lead

Coverage gap was worth 30 of 100 points in the fit score, and 37 of the 162 have no closed-days data in the workbook at all. Missing hours were scored as “no gap” rather than “unknown,” which pushed high-volume clinics with blank hours below the cut.

That is how Beauty Lab + Laser Murray — 3,701 reviews, the original target of this entire project — ended up with no email until this pass. Cosmetica, at 1,335 reviews, sat in the same bucket. Both are now written, and every row with missing hours is flagged so no email claims a closure that was never in the data.

Enrichment also found national chains sitting undetected inside what looked like a list of independent clinics. These need an enterprise motion, not the small-clinic pitch:

What is still missing

The SEO audit — set aside at your instruction

Still not done, and now deliberately parked rather than blocked. For the record the blocker is unchanged: the session’s network policy refuses page fetching for every domain in the list, and a control fetch of example.com fails identically. Title tags, canonicals, robots.txt, sitemaps, Core Web Vitals, schema and alt text all require loading the pages. No findings were invented to cover the gap.

No decision-maker names on any of the 804

Apollo people search returns API_INACCESSIBLE on the Free plan, and people_match enriches a person you can already name rather than discovering one. Credits cannot buy past this — it is a plan tier. Every email is written to accept a greeting line with no rework, so an upgrade converts all 804 at once.

Nothing verified against a live site

Every claim traces to the workbook or to search, never to the clinic’s own page. Confirm Kumi’s BBB rating and Suddenly Slimmer’s platform split before those two go out — both anchor their emails. Three domains in the source are wrong and bounce as supplied: the real ones are btyco.com, southbaymedicalspa.com and artistikbeauty.net. Another 104 Tier C leads carry an unverified domain by the workbook’s own admission.

Follow-up sequences

Every lead carries a five-touch sequence, not a single email. The spec is blunt about why: 55% of replies come from follow-ups, and 4–7 touch campaigns reply at 27% against 9% for one to three. Cadence widens as it goes — day 0, 3, 7, 14, 21, Tuesday to Thursday only — and each touch rotates to a genuinely new angle rather than bumping the last one.

Touch 1 is written for the individual business. Touches 2–5 come from a hand-written library keyed to eight sequence segments — coverage, reviews, qualification, membership, packages, multi-site, early, and wrong-fit — so a follow-up never repeats the argument the first email already made. The twelve wrong-fit leads get one touch and nothing after it.

One slot is deliberately not what the spec asks for

Touch 3 is supposed to be social proof — a case study from a similar company. You have no customers yet, so there is no case study to cite. Inventing one would put fabricated client results into 804 sequences, and a single prospect who asks “which clinic was that?” ends the conversation and the referral.

That slot instead carries verifiable product mechanics: bookings land as pending rather than confirmed, and clinical questions get refused outright. Both are true of the system as built. Replace touch 3 with a real case study the moment you close your first clinic — it is the single highest-leverage edit in the whole sequence.

The reply side is written too: REPLY_PLAYBOOK.md carries response copy for fourteen reply types, including the incumbent-tool objection, the HIPAA and BAA question, and the one this campaign is weakest on — “who else uses this?”

Phrases the spec flags as response-killers are absent throughout: no “just checking in,” no “following up on my previous message,” no “did you see my last email.” The breakup honors itself — if you send touch 5, do not contact that prospect again.

Before wave 1 — five things, in order

  1. Answer two questions in writing. What it costs, and whether you sign a BAA. Both get asked on the first good reply and neither can be improvised. They are marked as blanks in the reply playbook for exactly this reason.
  2. Warm a dedicated domain for three to four weeks. Not the domain the business runs on. SPF, DKIM and DMARC all passing, a physical address and a working unsubscribe in every email. The 20→40/day ramp in the schedule assumes this was done.
  3. Verify the 37 flagged addresses. Two minutes each. Montclair Rejuvenation Center alone is 1,398 reviews, the fourth-largest base in the workbook.
  4. Find real addresses for the best of the 22 broken ones. Ten minutes each for NakedMD, âme Spa and The Skin Center. Skip the rest.
  5. Then send in rank order, straight down MASTER_send_list.csv. It is already sequenced — wave, date and day of week per row.

At the benchmark rates in the directives — 4–5.8% reply, roughly half of them positive — 739 first touches plus follow-ups should produce 30 to 55 replies, 15 to 25 real conversations, and one to three clients. More than half of that arrives on the follow-ups, not the first email. REPLY_PLAYBOOK.md has response copy for all fourteen reply types, including the one this campaign is weakest on: “who else uses this?”

Two stop conditions worth memorising. Bounces above 4% in any wave, or a single spam complaint — Google’s threshold is 0.1%, which across 739 addresses is less than one. Either one means stop and fix the list before the next wave.

MASTER_send_list.csv is the file to work from — all 804 leads in send order, each with a hand-written first email, four follow-ups, a send status, a wave, a date, its warnings and its enrichment. REPLY_PLAYBOOK.md is what you open when someone answers. Everything else is provenance.
